"Strategy is not about being the best. It's about being unique — in a place where that uniqueness creates value."

— Roger Martin & A.G. Lafley, Playing to Win (2013)

Most early-stage failures aren't execution failures. They're strategy failures. The team built the right thing for the wrong person, or served the right person without a real reason to win. Roger Martin's Playing to Win gives you a repeatable cascade of five strategic choices — and the discipline to make them explicitly.

Here are the two that matter most at the early stage.

The strategic cascade

0

Winning aspiration

What does winning actually mean for us?

This is the first and highest-level choice in Martin's cascade — it sits above everything else. Not "what do we want to build" or "what market do we serve," but: what does it mean for us to genuinely win, from the perspective of both the customer and the company?

Martin is deliberate about the word winning. Most organizations set aspirations that are really just missions — vague statements like "be a great company" or "serve our customers well." These aren't winning aspirations because they don't force any real choices. A true winning aspiration must be specific enough that you could, in principle, know whether you achieved it or failed.

How real companies answered this ↘

P&G under Lafley — Martin's primary case study — didn't aspire to "make great consumer products." Their winning aspiration was to be the undisputed leader in every category they competed in, measured by market share and brand preference. That framing forced a hard rule: be number one or two, or exit. No comfortable middle.
Stripe's winning aspiration was to increase the GDP of the internet — to make it dramatically easier for any developer anywhere to build a business online. Ambitious enough to orient every product decision, specific enough to know when they were falling short of it.
The common trap for founders: skipping this step entirely and going straight to "where to play." The consequence is that later choices feel arbitrary — two co-founders can have completely different mental models of what success looks like and still agree on a target customer. The conflict surfaces when tradeoff decisions arrive.

Where to play

Which arena are you choosing to compete in?

Not "what does everyone need?" — but "where, specifically, do we show up?" This means choosing a customer segment, a geography, a channel, a price point, or a moment in the value chain — and committing to it. The goal isn't to be everywhere. It's to matter somewhere.

Most founders answer this too broadly. "SMBs" is not an arena. "SMBs in Southeast Asia using spreadsheets to manage inventory, with a single ops person and no budget for enterprise software" is an arena.

How real companies answered this ↘

Stripe chose developers — not CFOs, not enterprises, not payment consultants. Specifically: developers who found every existing payment API so painful they'd rather not support payments at all. A narrow wedge into a trillion-dollar market.
Figma chose browser-based design tooling for collaborative product teams. Not illustrators. Not enterprise CAD users. Not solo designers who wanted a desktop app. That specificity drove every architectural decision that followed.
Notion started with individual knowledge workers and small teams who felt trapped using five tools to do one job. Not enterprise IT buyers. Enterprise came later — once product-market fit was established from the bottom up.

2

How to win

What gives you the right to win in that arena?

Winning isn't just being better — it's being better for a reason that's hard to copy. Martin identifies two durable bases: cost leadership (you're structurally cheaper at scale) or differentiation (customers choose you even when you're not cheapest). For most startups, it's differentiation.

The harder question: differentiated on what, exactly? "Better UX" is not a right to win. "The only tool that works without an IT implementation" or "the only API a solo developer can integrate in an afternoon" — those are rights to win.

How real companies answered this ↘

Stripe's right to win: a 7-line integration and documentation so good it became a gold standard. They didn't win on price. They won because using Stripe was the most defensible technical decision a developer could make — and because developers love to recommend things they love.
Figma's right to win: multiplayer by default, zero install friction, the entire design asset living in a URL. These were architectural choices incumbents like Sketch couldn't reverse-engineer without rewriting everything. The moat was technical, not just UX.
Notion's right to win: radical flexibility through the block model — a single tool that could become anything — combined with a community that produced thousands of templates. The moat was network effects and switching cost, not features.

Capabilities

What must you be genuinely excellent at?

Once you know where to play and how to win, the capabilities you need aren't a guess — they follow logically. These are the 2–3 things you need to be world-class at to deliver on your differentiation. Not just "good product." Something specific, hard to build, and even harder to copy.

The test: write down the three things your product must be best-in-class at for your ideal customer to switch from whatever they use today. If you can't write them down specifically, your "where to play" is probably too vague — or your "how to win" doesn't hold up yet.

Systems

What structures reinforce your choices?

The companies that hold their position don't just make good choices — they build systems that make reversing those choices costly. For founders, this is often your hiring philosophy, pricing model, go-to-market motion, or the things you deliberately choose not to build.

Strategy is only real when it's reflected in what you do every day — who you hire, what you build, what you say no to, how you price, and which partnerships you pursue. If you can't trace a daily decision back to your "where to play" and "how to win," it might be the wrong decision.

The founder's test

Answer these five questions — in writing.

If any answer is vague, treat it as a red flag — not a planning problem, a strategy problem. The more specific your answers, the clearer your next product decision becomes.

Winning aspiration

What does winning look like for your company — specifically enough that you'd know if you achieved it or failed?

Where to play

Who, exactly, is your customer right now — and where do they live, work, or buy?

How to win

Why would that customer choose you over everything else they could do, including nothing?

Capabilities

What 2–3 things must you build or be that nobody in your arena can match?

Systems

What have you put in place that makes your position harder to copy over time?
